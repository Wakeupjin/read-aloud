Space Data Centers: A First-Principles Look

소리 내 읽으면 7분쯤 걸린다

낭독하는 법

이 원고는 정찬용 박사의 책 영어공부 절대로 하지 마라에 나오는 낭독법대로 읽는다. 아기가 모국어를 익히는 순서 그대로, 먼저 듣고 흉내 내고 소리 내 읽는다.

  1. 먼저 듣는다문단 앞 ▶를 눌러 원어민 소리를 듣는다. 뜻은 해석하지 않고 소리만 듣는다.
  2. 성대모사하듯 따라 읽는다큰 소리로 처음부터 끝까지 읽는다. 발음과 억양을 그대로 흉내 낸다. 쉼표에서 숨 쉬고, 앞에 앉은 사람에게 설명해 주듯 천천히 읽는다.
  3. 녹음해서 들어 본다읽을 때는 잘한 것 같아도, 녹음을 들으면 어색한 곳이 들린다. 그 문단만 다시 듣고 다시 읽는다. 입에 붙었다 싶으면 끝낸다.
  • 다 이해 안 돼도 괜찮다. 같은 글을 몇 번 소리 내 읽다 보면 뜻이 저절로 잡힌다.
  • 모르는 단어는 찾지 않는다. 자주 나오는 단어는 결국 뇌가 알아서 익힌다. 정 궁금한 것만 영영사전으로 보고, 한국어로 번역하지 않는다.
  • 외우지 않는다. 소리 내 읽기만 해도 머리에 남고, 필요할 때 입으로 나온다.
  • 일주일에 하루는 영어를 쉰다.
왜 이렇게 읽나

우리는 한국어를 문법책이나 단어장으로 익히지 않았다. 몇 년을 듣고 흉내 내고 말하다 보니, 어느새 머리보다 혀가 먼저 말하게 됐다.

정찬용 박사는 이걸 체화라고 부른다. 자전거나 수영처럼 몸이 익히는 거라, 한동안 쉬어도 다시 하면 된다. 외운 단어는 시간이 지나면 잊히지만, 입에 붙은 소리는 남는다.

나도 이 방법으로 영어를 익혔다. 박사님께 배우면서 처음엔 듣기만 했고, 그다음엔 원서를 소리 내 읽으며 걸어 다녔다.

방법 출처: 정찬용 박사의 영어공부 절대로 하지 마라와 그 개정판. 설명은 책 내용을 바탕으로 직접 정리했다. 원고 음성은 AI 음성이다.

내 낭독 저장

Every time you ask an AI a question, a computer somewhere does the work. These computers live in data centers. A data center is a huge building full of computers. It needs a lot of electricity, and a big cooling system to keep the computers from overheating.

AI is growing fast, so we need more and more data centers. But there's a problem. It's getting hard to find enough electricity for them. So some people have a bold idea: let's put data centers in space. Is that smart, or just a dream? To find out, I used first-principles thinking.

What is first-principles thinking?

It's a way to solve problems. You break a problem down into its most basic facts. Then you build your answer up from there. The idea goes back to Aristotle, and Elon Musk made it famous.

Here's his favorite example. People used to say batteries were expensive, and always would be. Musk asked a simple question: what is a battery made of? Some metals and a few other materials. Then he checked their prices. The materials cost far less than the battery. So batteries could get much cheaper, and they did.

Like Socrates, you keep asking one question: is that really true? Let's ask it about space.

The answer first

Space is not exciting because electricity is cheap there. It's exciting because you can get power without land, without power lines, and without waiting for permission. And here's a surprise. Many people think space is cold, so cooling is easy. That's wrong. Cooling is the hardest part.

Step one: break it down

Imagine an AI data center costs 100 dollars. About 60 dollars goes to the computer chips. About 30 goes to the building, the power equipment, and the cooling system. Only about 7 goes to the electricity bill. The last 3 pays for people and repairs.

But money is not the only problem. Time is a problem too. You can buy chips quickly, but connecting a new building to the power grid can take years. A chip with no power is just an expensive piece of metal.

Step two: move each piece to space

The electricity bill almost disappears. In space, there are no clouds, and in the right orbit, there's almost no night. So a solar panel there can make up to eight times more power than on the ground. Sounds great! But electricity was only 7 dollars out of 100. Making it free saves just 7 dollars.

The building disappears too. But now you need solar panels, cooling panels, a satellite to hold everything, and a rocket to carry it all up. Today, that costs about four and a half times more than a similar setup on the ground.

The chips get harder to take care of. Space is full of invisible radiation that can cause errors in computer chips. And if a chip breaks, nobody can go up and fix it. So you have to send extra chips, just in case.

Step three: the real advantages

So why are people still excited? There are four reasons.

First, the bottleneck changes. A bottleneck is the narrow neck of a bottle. It slows everything down. On Earth, the bottleneck is power: waiting for power lines, for permission, and for neighbors who say "not here." In space, the problem becomes building rockets and satellites. That's an engineering problem, and engineering problems usually get cheaper the more you do them. This is the biggest advantage.

Second, you don't need water, land, or your neighbors' approval.

Third, AI chips get old fast anyway. Companies replace them about every five years, like we replace our phones. A satellite is also built to last about five years. So not being able to fix it matters less than you'd think. At the end, it falls back and burns up in the sky, like a shooting star.

Fourth, some data is already in space. Satellites that take pictures of Earth make huge amounts of data every day. Sending it all down is like sending a whole movie library over slow Wi-Fi. So it's smarter to look at the pictures up there, and send down only the answer, like "There's a fire here."

The three walls

Wall one: cooling. On Earth, a fan blows the heat away, like blowing on hot soup. But space has no air. Think of a thermos. It keeps coffee hot because its walls hold empty space. In space, a computer is like that coffee. The only way for the heat to escape is to glow it away, like a hot stove giving off warmth. For that, you need huge cooling panels. Huge panels are heavy, and heavy things cost a lot to launch. So the cooling problem becomes a rocket problem.

Wall two: launch cost. Everything goes up by rocket, and you pay by weight, like shipping a package. Today, sending one kilogram into space costs about 3,600 dollars. To make space data centers worth it, that has to drop to about 200 dollars. That's about 18 times cheaper. How? Reuse the rocket. Imagine an airline that threw away the plane after every flight. Tickets would cost a fortune. SpaceX's Starship is built to fly again and again. To reach 200 dollars, it may need to fly about 180 times a year. That's about once every two days.

Wall three: teamwork. There are two jobs in AI: training it, and using it. Training is like a whole class writing one giant textbook together. Thousands of chips must check each other's work all the time, so they need super-fast connections. On Earth, they sit side by side, linked by fast cables. In space, they sit on different satellites and talk with laser beams, which is still much slower. Using an AI is different. It's like opening the finished textbook to answer one question. A small group of chips, like the ones on a single satellite, can do that alone. So for now, space is good for using AI, but not for training it.

Where are we now?

In October, Google sent its first test satellite into space. It's tiny: just four AI chips. It works, but it can only run for about fifteen minutes. Then it has to stop and cool down. Elon Musk says space will be the cheapest place for AI in two to three years. Jeff Bezos thinks it could take up to twenty.

In one sentence

Space data centers are not about free electricity. They trade one problem, getting power on Earth, for another problem, launch cost, which keeps getting cheaper. So watch two numbers: the cost of sending one kilogram into space, and the weight of the cooling panels.
