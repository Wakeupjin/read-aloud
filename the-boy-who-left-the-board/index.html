낭독 원고 4

The Boy Who Left the Board

소리 내 읽으면 4분쯤 걸린다

낭독하는 법

이 원고는 정찬용 박사의 책 영어공부 절대로 하지 마라에 나오는 낭독법대로 읽는다. 아기가 모국어를 익히는 순서 그대로, 먼저 듣고 흉내 내고 소리 내 읽는다.

  1. 먼저 듣는다문단 앞 ▶를 눌러 원어민 소리를 듣는다. 뜻은 해석하지 않고 소리만 듣는다.
  2. 성대모사하듯 따라 읽는다큰 소리로 처음부터 끝까지 읽는다. 발음과 억양을 그대로 흉내 낸다. 쉼표에서 숨 쉬고, 앞에 앉은 사람에게 설명해 주듯 천천히 읽는다.
  3. 녹음해서 들어 본다읽을 때는 잘한 것 같아도, 녹음을 들으면 어색한 곳이 들린다. 그 문단만 다시 듣고 다시 읽는다. 입에 붙었다 싶으면 끝낸다.
  • 다 이해 안 돼도 괜찮다. 같은 글을 몇 번 소리 내 읽다 보면 뜻이 저절로 잡힌다.
  • 모르는 단어는 찾지 않는다. 자주 나오는 단어는 결국 뇌가 알아서 익힌다. 정 궁금한 것만 영영사전으로 보고, 한국어로 번역하지 않는다.
  • 외우지 않는다. 소리 내 읽기만 해도 머리에 남고, 필요할 때 입으로 나온다.
  • 일주일에 하루는 영어를 쉰다.
왜 이렇게 읽나

우리는 한국어를 문법책이나 단어장으로 익히지 않았다. 몇 년을 듣고 흉내 내고 말하다 보니, 어느새 머리보다 혀가 먼저 말하게 됐다.

정찬용 박사는 이걸 체화라고 부른다. 자전거나 수영처럼 몸이 익히는 거라, 한동안 쉬어도 다시 하면 된다. 외운 단어는 시간이 지나면 잊히지만, 입에 붙은 소리는 남는다.

나도 이 방법으로 영어를 익혔다. 박사님께 배우면서 처음엔 듣기만 했고, 그다음엔 원서를 소리 내 읽으며 걸어 다녔다.

방법 출처: 정찬용 박사의 영어공부 절대로 하지 마라와 그 개정판. 설명은 책 내용을 바탕으로 직접 정리했다. 원고 음성은 AI 음성이다.

내 낭독 저장

A day in the mountains

Somewhere in the mountains of Liechtenstein, in the late 1980s, a boy of about twelve sat hunched over a chessboard. He was one of the strongest young players in England. The game in front of him ground on for hours, through the afternoon and into the evening, and at the end of it he lost.

Losing was not the strange part. The strange part was what he did next. As he thought about the hall around him, he saw hundreds of brilliant minds bent over little wooden squares, burning an entire day of thought on a game. He began to wonder whether this was really the best use of a mind like his, or of any mind at all.

The boy was Demis Hassabis. He had learned chess at four by watching his father and uncle play. By thirteen he was a master. And yet, not long after that day in the mountains, he stopped treating chess as the destination. It became a training ground instead.

A strange list

What came next reads like a list someone made up. At seventeen he helped build Theme Park, a video game in which players ran their own amusement park, and it sold millions of copies. He studied computer science at Cambridge and started his own game studio. Then, in his late twenties, he did something few game designers ever do. He went back to school to study the human brain.

A boy named Ender

It was around that time, at about thirty, that he picked up a science fiction novel called Ender's Game. Orson Scott Card wrote it in 1985. It tells the story of Ender Wiggin, a six-year-old boy taken from his family and sent to a military school orbiting the Earth. Humanity is terrified of a second alien invasion, and the adults believe only a child genius can lead the defense. So they train Ender through games: mock battles in zero gravity, simulations, tests that grow harder and lonelier every year. His teachers keep him isolated on purpose. They want a commander, not a happy child.

In the final test, Ender plays what he believes is just another simulation. He wins brilliantly. Only afterward does he learn the truth. The game was real. The battle was real. He had won an actual war, and the weight of that victory would stay with him for the rest of his life.

Hassabis saw himself in Ender. Decades later, the writer Sebastian Mallaby sat down with him in London to begin a book about his life. Before anything else, Hassabis gave him an assignment: read Ender's Game first.

A different board

It is not hard to see why. Both stories have a gifted boy, set apart from others, trained through games for a task far larger than any game. For Ender, the game turned out to be the war. For Hassabis, chess and video games turned out to be practice for something else entirely: teaching machines to think. In 2016, his company's program AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol at the ancient game of Go. A few years later, another of its systems cracked a fifty-year-old puzzle in biology by predicting the shapes of proteins. In 2024, that work won him a Nobel Prize.

The boy who left the chessboard never really stopped playing. He just changed the board.
