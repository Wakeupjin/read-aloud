The big idea

After the 2026 elections in the United States, people asked whether the AI boom is in trouble. The short answer is no. Companies still want huge amounts of AI computing. What has changed is that building the places for it is getting harder, slower, and more expensive. The winners will be the ones who can actually get buildings running, not the ones who announce the biggest plans.

Think of apartments

The tenants are Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI, and Anthropic. They need a lot of computing space, and they rent much of it instead of building it all themselves.

The property managers are companies called neoclouds, like CoreWeave. They fill the space with Nvidia chips and rent out the computing power.

The builders are the landlords. They buy land, get electricity and water connected, and put up the buildings.

Nvidia sells the furniture, which means the chips. It also invests in the property managers, and sometimes promises to rent any empty units itself. So the same rent money can show up on several companies' books: as chip sales, as cloud revenue, and as lease income. Don't add those up as if they were separate demand.

What the election changed

State governors largely decide whether these buildings get approved. Many voters are angry that data centers can push up electricity bills and use a lot of water. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa won the race for governor, the first Democrat to win it in about thirty years. She promised to pause new data center approvals until the state passes laws to protect residents.

The surprising part is that the party doesn't predict the outcome. Some Republican states still welcome data centers, but they want builders to bring their own power and pay for their own grid and water upgrades. Everywhere, the message sounds about the same: build here if you like, but our residents won't pay for it.

Why a big number isn't money yet

Saying "we have one gigawatt" is like saying "we own land for a thousand apartments." It's a start, not a paycheck. Think of it as a ladder with five steps.

Step one, you own land and have a plan. Step two, the power company has promised to connect you. Step three, a tenant has signed a lease. Step four, the building is finished and has power. Step five, the tenant has moved in and is paying rent.

Only step five is real cash. A contract worth tens of billions of dollars usually means rent paid over about fifteen years, and only if everything gets built on time.

Who benefits

When new building gets harder, buildings that already work become more valuable, the way existing apartments rent for more during a housing shortage. Newer projects face delays and extra costs. Some companies build their own power plants on site instead of waiting for the grid. That's like installing your own generator. It helps, but it needs its own permits and money.

Three questions to ask

Is the customer real, and can it pay? Where is the building, and do the local politicians and residents support it? Can the signed contract actually turn into steady cash?
